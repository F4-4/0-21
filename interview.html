Explain a time you had to ship with limited resources

4-person team, 10yr legacy debt • cut to Send/Receive/Buy/Sell only • invisible background migration pipeline • staged rollout 5%→10%→20% w/ downgrade path • 28X faster load, +7% conversion, ~£1.2M/qtr, legacy fully deprecated

The blockchain.com web wallet rewrite — 4 people, 10 years of debt, zero room for lost user funds.

Situation: A decade-old web wallet was riddled with technical debt, running 20s+ load times, and structurally blocked from new revenue. It couldn't just be shut down — a large, self-custodial user segment relied on it, so any downtime risked locking people out of their own funds.

Task: Rebuild it from vanilla JS to React/TypeScript, migrate every user's account safely, with a core team of four — me, one EM, one Staff Engineer, one Mid-level Engineer. No room to hire our way out of the constraint.

Action: I made resourcing the forcing function for scope, not an excuse for slower delivery. We cut to the non-negotiable path — Send, Receive, Buy, Sell — and deferred everything else until that was safely live. We shipped a background, invisible account-migration pipeline so users never had to take action, and used a staged rollout (5% → 10% → 20%) with an explicit downgrade path so risk was capped at every stage, not just at the end.

Result: 28X faster load times, +7% trading conversion, ~£1.2M in additional quarterly volume, and full deprecation of the legacy system — delivered by a team a fraction of the size a project like this would normally get.

Why this lands for senior/principal: it shows you treat constraints as a design input, not a blocker — and that "limited resources" forced sharper prioritisation rather than lower quality.

How do you reflect?

Wallet: exec pushed 10%→20% before feedback actioned → ticket spike → rollback → 'moving fast without guardrails is false speed.' Tokenisation: Etherlink-exclusive capped TVL ceiling, cross-chain could've been 5x — surface it yourself before someone else finds it.

I look for the decision that could have gone differently, not just the outcome — wins included.

The clearest example: during the wallet rollout, executive pressure pushed us from 10% to 20% traffic before we'd fully actioned early user feedback, and support tickets spiked. I rolled back to 10%, fixed the root cause, and only then resumed. The reflection wasn't "we made a mistake" — it was "moving fast without guardrails is false speed," and that's stuck with me as an operating principle since.

Same instinct on tokenisation: after the fact, I was honest that keeping the assets Etherlink-exclusive capped our TVL ceiling — cross-chain issuance could plausibly have grown it 5x. I'd rather surface that trade-off myself than have someone else find it.

Pattern to keep hitting in interviews: name the pressure that pushed toward the wrong call, name what you did about it, name what changed in how you operate after.

Tell me about a product strategy you built from scratch

Uranium.io: no on-chain market existed • $4.2M physical lot size locked out retail/mid-market • uranium outperforming S&P 500 • validated via macro capital-demand signal, not user research • chose physically-backed over synthetic (trust > speed) • $14M TVL, 75K+ transfers → scaled into Metals.io

Uranium.io — anchoring an MVP on a pure macro-capital thesis, with no traditional user research, because the market signal made it redundant.

Situation: No on-chain market existed for uranium exposure. Physical uranium trading required a ~$4.2M minimum lot size, locking retail and mid-market allocators out entirely — while spot uranium was aggressively outperforming the S&P 500 on the back of energy shocks and AI power demand.

Task: Decide whether and how to bring a genuinely novel asset class on-chain, with no playbook to copy.

Action: Instead of consumer interviews, I validated against macro capital-demand signals — the asymmetry between outperformance and access barriers was the evidence. I then made the highest-leverage strategic call early: physically-backed instead of synthetic, trading short-term complexity for institutional trust and audit-anchored solvency, specifically to capture allocators who wouldn't touch derivative counterparty risk.

Result: $14M TVL, 75K+ on-chain transfers, and a validated thesis that justified scaling horizontally into a multi-asset platform (Metals.io).

How do you evaluate whether to build, buy, or partner?

Considered acquiring a wallet product for its user base • modelled real cost-per-reactivatable-user, not headline valuation • too expensive vs mostly-dormant base • recommended no — twice, a year apart, same answer both times • partner examples: Bakkt, Li.Fi, VNX/Noemon (regulatory/custody risk, no differentiation upside) vs built Permit2 in-house (real gap, no off-shelf option)

M&A evaluation of acquiring a crypto wallet product for its user base — said no twice, a year apart, once the real cost-per-reactivatable-user was modelled against valuation.

Situation: The business considered acquiring another crypto wallet product, primarily to inherit its existing user base rather than build audience from zero — we recognised organic growth has its own real challenges and cost.

Task: I worked with M&A on the investigation, writing up a summary document covering valuation and the outcomes needed to justify the deal going ahead.

Action: Rather than evaluating against headline valuation alone, I modelled the realistic cost per user we'd actually be able to reactivate — the majority of the target's user base was unlikely to come back. That made the true acquisition cost per usable user far higher than the deal price implied.

Result: Recommended against it — too expensive relative to a mostly-dormant user base. The target came back a year later at a lower valuation, but by then we'd already built out our own product further and more of their user base had dropped off, so the economics were still unfavourable. Remained a no both times.

Broader framework I use for build/buy/partner: is this core to our differentiation or commodity infrastructure? What's the real economics (not headline cost)? What's the speed-to-market vs control trade-off? On the infra side specifically: partnered with Bakkt for execution in states we couldn't operate in directly, Li.Fi for cross-chain liquidity routing, and VNX/Noemon Tech as regulated issuers for gold and rare earths — all partner calls where building in-house would have meant taking on regulatory or custody risk with no differentiation upside. Conversely, built the Permit2 payment facilitator in-house on x402 because no off-the-shelf stack supported non-EIP-3009 tokens — that gap was exactly where owning the build mattered.

A strong disagreement with an Engineering Lead on approach vs. timeline

Two pushbacks on same EM: (1) ambitious scope commit → argued for realistic external commitment + buffer, spare capacity to bonus features if unused (2) ship-on-cadence despite obvious unresolved perf issues → pushed to delay rather than ship with 'fix coming soon' • both calls accepted by stakeholders, avoided overcommit miss + under-polished release

Two separate disagreements with my EM on the Trilitech wallet — ambitious scope commitments vs realistic buffer, and holding a release for polish vs keeping cadence.

Situation: My EM was ambitious in two ways I didn't fully agree with: he wanted to commit to an ambitious feature scope for an upcoming cycle, and separately wanted to ship a scheduled release on cadence despite obvious, unresolved performance issues in the app.

Task: Push back on both without undermining the EM or losing the team's delivery credibility — balancing his instinct for ambition and consistency against my own experience of what happens when either backfires.

Action: On scope, I argued for communicating a more realistic commitment externally, intentionally leaving room for bugs, with any spare capacity going toward bonus features if the buffer wasn't needed — rather than committing the ambitious version and hoping nothing broke. On the release, I pushed to delay rather than ship on schedule: the performance issues were obvious, and I'd rather hold the release than put it in front of users with a "fix coming soon" promise attached. I communicated both calls directly to stakeholders with the reasoning, rather than letting the EM's original plan run and hoping it worked out.

Result: Stakeholders accepted both calls — reasonable, while still maintaining real pressure to deliver. We avoided both an overcommitment miss and shipping a visibly under-polished release.

This is a good complement to the exec-disagreement story specifically because it's peer-level, not up-the-chain — shows you'll hold a line with someone you work with daily, not just in front of leadership.

Walk me through handling a critical production incident / post-mortem

Detect: ticket spike at 20% cohort = trigger to investigate immediately. Contain: rolled back 20%→10% to cap blast radius. Root cause: unresolved issue from earlier cohort's feedback, scaled past too soon. Fix & resume: only scaled again once actually resolved. Process: gates now tied to 'root cause resolved,' not 'cohort looks stable.'

The wallet migration rollback, reframed around incident structure: detect, contain, root-cause, update the guardrail.

Detect: A spike in support tickets at the 20% rollout cohort was the signal — I treated an unusual pattern in real user-facing data as the trigger to investigate immediately, not something to monitor passively.

Contain: Rather than trying to patch forward while more users were exposed, I led an immediate rollback from 20% back to 10% to cap the blast radius while the actual cause was investigated.

Root cause: Identified the specific unresolved issue from the earlier cohort's feedback that had been prematurely scaled past before it was fully actioned.

Fix & resume: Fixed the root cause, then only resumed scaling once it was actually resolved — not once time had simply passed.

Process update: This experience directly shaped how I ran every subsequent rollout stage on that project — scaling gates were tied to "root cause resolved," not just "cohort looks stable," so the same failure mode couldn't recur silently.

If asked for a formal post-mortem structure specifically: I'd always want a blameless writeup covering timeline, detection method, impact/blast radius, root cause, immediate fix, and the specific guardrail or process change that prevents recurrence — not just the fix itself.

Tell me about a time a project went off track due to a capability gap

Mobile app team had no crypto/scalable-consumer-app background → EM's client-side-only architecture (no backend data layer) broke at 5 concurrent test users • design also new to mobile, compounded gap • recognised: not a coaching problem, a genuine capability gap → pushed for full rewrite + backed hiring a specialist to lead technical direction rather than upskilling under deadline

An engineering and design team with no crypto or scalable-consumer-app background made architecture assumptions that broke under real load — I had to recognise it wasn't fixable through coaching, and push to bring in someone who could actually close the gap.

Situation: On a mobile app build, the engineering team had no prior crypto background and no experience with scalable consumer applications. The EM's architecture assumed everything could be front-loaded on the client side, with no backend layer to handle data loading — a reasonable-looking call that turned out to be structurally wrong. I backed hiring someone new specifically to fill that knowledge gap and take over the technical direction, rather than trying to upskill under a live deadline.

Result: The rewrite addressed the root architectural flaw instead of patching symptoms, and closing the experience gap directly (via hiring) fixed the recurring pattern rather than just the immediate performance issue.

The sharpest point to make if asked a follow-up: the mistake wasn't the EM's original assumption — inexperienced teams misjudge architecture, that's normal. The judgment call was recognising early that this needed a structural fix (new capability) rather than a series of patches, and being willing to say so before it cost more time.

Walk me through how a blockchain transaction actually gets confirmed

Signed (private key, no key exposed) → broadcast to mempool → validators/miners check validity & bundle into a candidate block → block added to chain (PoW/PoS) → 'confirmed,' though finality in practice means waiting several more blocks on top • L2s (e.g. Etherlink) batch transactions and periodically settle back to L1, inheriting its security

Signed → broadcast → picked up by validators/miners → included in a block → confirmed as the chain builds on top of it.

At a high level: a user signs a transaction with their private key, proving authenticity without revealing the key itself. It's broadcast to the network's mempool, where validators (or miners, on proof-of-work chains) pick it up, check it's valid (correct signature, sufficient balance/gas), and bundle it with other transactions into a candidate block. Once that block is added to the chain — via proof-of-work mining or proof-of-stake validation depending on the network — the transaction is technically confirmed, but "finality" in practice means waiting for several additional blocks to be built on top, so the transaction becomes computationally impractical to reverse. On L2s like Etherlink, transactions are typically batched and settled back to the L1 periodically, which is why L2 costs and speeds differ from L1 while inheriting its underlying security.

Crypto technical staples: rollups, L1 vs L2, MEV, and what an audit actually checks

L1 vs L2: L1 = independent consensus (Ethereum, Bitcoin); L2 = off-chain processing, periodic settlement back to L1, inherits its security (Etherlink, Arbitrum, Optimism). Rollup: zk (validity proof, fast finality) vs optimistic (fraud-proof window, ~7 days). MEV: value extracted via tx ordering/front-running/sandwich attacks; mitigated by private mempools, batch auctions, slippage protection. Audit: checks known vuln classes (reentrancy, overflow, access-control, oracle manipulation) + spec match — reduces risk, doesn't eliminate it.

Quick, correct definitions to have ready — these come up as rapid-fire technical checks more than deep-dive questions.

L1 vs L2: an L1 (e.g. Ethereum, Bitcoin) is a base blockchain with its own independent validator/miner set and consensus. An L2 (e.g. Etherlink, Arbitrum, Optimism) processes transactions off the L1 for speed and cost, then periodically settles a summary back to the L1 — inheriting the L1's security guarantees without needing its own independent trust set.

Rollup: a specific type of L2 that executes transactions off-chain, then posts either a validity proof (zk-rollup — cryptographically proves correctness) or the raw transaction data plus a fraud-proof window (optimistic rollup — assumes correctness unless challenged) back to the L1. The difference matters for withdrawal speed: zk-rollups can finalize fast since the proof is verified immediately; optimistic rollups need a challenge window (often ~7 days) before funds are considered final.

MEV (Maximal Extractable Value): profit a block producer (or a searcher paying them) can extract by choosing transaction order, insertion, or exclusion within a block — most visibly through front-running or sandwich attacks on DEX trades. Relevant to a wallet/product PM because MEV exposure is a real, often invisible cost to users that good product design (private mempools, batch auctions, slippage protection) can mitigate.

Smart contract audit: a manual and tool-assisted review checking for known vulnerability classes (reentrancy, integer overflow/underflow, access-control gaps, oracle manipulation, logic errors against the spec) plus a check that the contract does what the documentation claims it does. An audit reduces risk, it doesn't eliminate it — worth being able to say this plainly rather than overselling what an audit guarantees.
